## TL;DR

Komisar argues that a large share of childhood mental illness (anxiety, depression, ADHD, behavioral problems) is preventable if society prioritizes **presence**, **prioritization of children**, and **prevention** rather than symptom management. She frames many ADHD presentations as **stress responses** (amygdala hyperactivation, underdeveloped hippocampal “off switch”) and stresses two critical neurodevelopment windows: **0–3** and **adolescence (roughly 9–25)**. On technology, she advocates strict limits—especially before age two—and warns that screens and social media spike dopamine and chronic stress in developing brains.

> **Note:** These are notes from a popular podcast, not medical advice. Komisar’s views—especially on daycare and gendered parenting roles—are debated in the research community. Use this document as a starting point for further reading, including the linked PDF and primary sources.

## Core framework: presence, prioritization, prevention

1. **Presence** — Physical and emotional availability during critical brain-building periods (0–3 and adolescence).
2. **Prioritization** — Children’s emotional needs placed ahead of careerism, individualism, and convenience.
3. **Prevention** — Address root stressors before medicating or managing symptoms alone.

Komisar cites roughly **one in five** children (US) developing a serious mental illness before adulthood, and criticizes a mental-health model she compares to “cutting the grass”—treating symptoms without asking what caused the stress.

---

## Critical development windows

| Window | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| **0–3 years** | Rapid right-brain and emotional-regulation development; attachment security formed through consistent, sensitive caregiving. |
| **9–25 (adolescence)** | Second major plasticity window; social, academic, and digital pressures hit a still-maturing prefrontal cortex. |

Missing the first window does not mean permanent damage—repair is possible in adolescence and through reparative relationships (therapy, attuned parenting)—but early investment “fortifies” children for later adversity.

## Attachment

**Secure attachment** (consistent, sensitive care) supports resilience and healthier adult relationships.

**Insecure patterns** (often discussed in the episode):

| Style | Early experience (simplified) | Adult tendencies (simplified) |
|-------|------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Avoidant** | Caregiver emotionally distant | Difficulty with intimacy, withdrawal |
| **Ambivalent** | Inconsistent availability | Anxiety, clinginess in relationships |
| **Disorganized** | Frightening or chaotic caregiving | Volatility, self-harm risk, relationship chaos |

Komisar ties early attachment insecurity to later anxiety, depression, ADHD-like symptoms, and personality difficulties—not as sole cause, but as a major risk factor in longitudinal attachment research she cites.

## ADHD as stress response (not “disorder” in her framing)

Key claims from the episode:

- ADHD is often a **manifestation of chronic stress**, not a fixed neurobiological disorder in every case.
- Stress activates the **amygdala** (“on switch”); the **hippocampus** helps turn stress off. She describes many stressed children as having a large, overactive amygdala and a small hippocampus—**“gas, no brakes.”**
- Triggers include family conflict, separation, learning struggles, **overstimulation**, and **technology**.
- Treatment should start with **curiosity about stressors**, not only medication (which she calls symptom suppression).

---

## Daycare and early separation

Komisar’s position (contested elsewhere):

- Babies need a **primary attachment figure** (she emphasizes mothers biologically for soothing/regulation) for emotional security in the first three years.
- **Daycare before age three** may increase cortisol, aggression, behavioral problems, and attachment difficulties—she argues it is **not** ideal “socialization” for infants.
- **Quality vs. quantity time** is a myth: children need presence **on their schedule**, not only when parents are available after work.
- Alternatives she discusses: kinship care, staggered parental schedules, creative economic workarounds—not always accessible to all families.

---

## Mothers vs. fathers (biological framing)

She distinguishes roles using hormones and evolution:

- **Mothers / oxytocin** — Sensitive nurturing, soothing, skin-to-skin contact, emotional regulation.
- **Fathers / vasopressin** — Playful, tactile stimulation, exploration, excitement/aggression regulation.

Both parents matter; she rejects raising a child without either parent as “missing a piece.” This framing is politically and scientifically debated; treat as one lens, not consensus.

## Technology and screens (Unpluged-Al focus)

**Timestamp:** ~2:22:23–2:27:11 in the full episode.

### Guidelines she endorses

| Age | Guidance |
|-----|----------|
| **Under 2** | **No technology** per AAP-style guidance—no iPhones, iPads. Co-watching gentle content (e.g. *Mr. Rogers*) at age two together is acceptable. |
| **After 2** | **Strict regulation**; screens are not neutral babysitters. |
| **Adolescents** | Especially careful with **social media**—perfectionism, comparison, fear states. |

### Mechanisms she describes

1. **Dopamine** — Tech raises dopamine; adolescent brains may respond **~10×** more strongly than adults (analogy: same “dose” hits much harder).
2. **Amygdala activation** — Chronic stress from feeds, comparison, and FOMO; hyper-vigilance and fear.
3. **Addiction** — Harder for teens to separate from devices once hooked.
4. **Social media on teen girls** — Self-consciousness and perfectionism exploited by product design; “Inception”-like stuck virtual reality of inadequacy.

### Replacement

Nothing replaces **parental presence**—co-regulation, conversation, physical play. Technology cannot substitute for attachment figures.

## Other themes (brief)

- **Declining birth rates** — Economic pressure, infertility, environmental factors, loss of extended family support.
- **Epigenetics** — “Sensitivity” genes; responsive early care may prevent expression of vulnerability.
- **ACE scores** — Childhood adversity correlates with ADHD risk; poverty and stress compound.
- **Young men** — Identity, purpose, declining testosterone; need for balanced gender roles and mentorship.
- **Employers** — Should support parental leave and flexible work so parents can be present.

## Counterpoints (for balanced reading)

Komisar’s daycare and maternal-role emphasis are not universally accepted. Critics note:

- Longitudinal childcare research often finds **family dynamics** and quality of care matter more than setting alone.
- Many children in quality daycare thrive; socioeconomic access and caregiver ratios are key variables.
- ADHD has multifactorial causes; medication helps many children when appropriately diagnosed.

Read the DOAC PDF and peer-reviewed attachment/childcare literature alongside this episode.
